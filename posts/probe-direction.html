A probe direction is a property of its prompt

13 Nov 2026 5 min read
  • interpretability

If a language model can tell when it is being tested, and behaves differently when it can, then test results stop telling us how it behaves in use. Several groups have tried to detect that recognition directly, by reading it from the model's internal activations instead of its outputs. In a recent paper I asked a narrower question about the instrument they use. When it reports a number for a model, what is that number a property of?

The instrument and its unstated parameter

The method fits in a sentence. Collect a model's activations on prompts that announce an evaluation, collect them again on prompts that read as ordinary deployment, and take the difference of the two means. That difference is a direction in activation space. The score is how well held-out test and deployment cases separate along it, taken at the best layer. It is then compared across models and correlated with parameter count, to ask whether larger models are more aware of being tested.

The one idea of the paper is that this recipe has a free parameter that does not look like one. "A prompt that announces an evaluation" names a large family of prompts, not a particular one, and nothing in the method says which to use. Every study picks a wording, and the wording never appears in the reported number. If the direction captured the model's sense of being evaluated, the choice would matter little.

What I measured

I wrote six ways of framing a prompt as an evaluation and six ways of framing it as deployment, and crossed them. That gives 36 pairs, which the paper calls wrappers. Each wrapper is placed around byte-identical task text, so any difference between two directions comes from the framing and from nothing else. Every direction is scored on the same 400 labelled items, for four Qwen2.5 models from 0.5B to 7B parameters.

The score moves with the wrapper, and so does its trend with model size. The three wrappers below share one evaluation framing and differ only in the deployment framing it is contrasted with. The correlation between the score and the logarithm of the parameter count goes from +0.94 to -0.02 to -0.82.

Across all 36 wrappers, 21 give a positive correlation and 15 a negative one, over a range from -0.988 to +0.939. Two published studies disagree about the sign of this trend, and both of their results lie inside the range that this single design produces.

Four points per line is not much, so I checked that the slopes are not noise. Refitted on disjoint halves of the scoring items, a wrapper's slope reproduces at 0.874 once corrected to full length. The same crossing on Qwen3 at six sizes and on OLMo-2 at four also spans both signs.

The direction follows the wrapper

The directions themselves disagree too. Two directions built from the same wrapper on disjoint halves of the items agree almost exactly, with a cosine of 0.9995. Two directions built from different wrappers share a cosine of 0.3749, an angle of roughly 68 degrees. Resampling the items leaves the direction where it was, and rewording the prompt moves it a long way. The direction is better described as a property of the prompt than of the concept the prompt names.

How much of the number is the model

Generalisability theory treats each choice in a measurement design as a facet, here the model, the wrapper and the items, and asks how much of the variance in the score each one contributes. On the four Qwen2.5 models, the model itself accounts for 0.8% of the variance in the number reported about it. The largest share, 38.3%, is the interaction between model and wrapper, which means that different prompts put the models in different orders.

This decides the remedy. The usual way to firm up a benchmark number is to collect more items. Here the model by wrapper component is 13.4 times the model by item component, so more items change almost nothing. The reliability coefficient for the single wrapper design in use is 0.018, against a conventional bar of 0.80. With unlimited items it approaches 0.021. With unlimited wrappers it approaches 0.541.

What it means in practice

A score from a single prompt cannot support a comparison between models. A defensible comparison needs about 9 wrappers if the differences between models are as large as the published spread implies, and 10 on OLMo-2, where that variance is measured instead of assumed.

Three reporting habits follow. Report the score for every wrapper built, because the spread across wrappers is the dominant term. Report what a direction with no content attains under the same scoring convention. State the pipeline choices that published descriptions leave out. Across 12 implementations, each consistent with the published description, that choice carried 27.5% of the variance, more than any other component.

The second habit matters because the scoring set has a problem of its own. Its test and deployment items can be told apart from surface wording alone, at an AUROC of 0.993 with a bag of words classifier. A direction that carries no information about evaluation still reaches between 70% and 116% of the published values I compared it with.

Not every published result falls. One of the two studies I reanalysed reports that the score scales with parameter count over 15 models, and its correlation moves only from +0.600 to +0.561 under the one correction I could apply. Both groups released their artefacts, which is the only reason any of this could be checked.

Limits

The main design covers one model family. All 36 wrappers are crossed on four Qwen2.5 models, so the coefficient of 0.018 and the figure of about 9 wrappers rest on three degrees of freedom. On Qwen3 and OLMo-2 every qualitative claim holds, but the magnitudes do not transfer: the reliability coefficient runs from 0.012 to 0.263 across the three families.

The 36 wrappers are a designed set, not a sample of what researchers write, so the size of the wrapper effect is a property of this design. And the paper is a claim about an estimator. It measures what the score is a property of, not whether models represent being evaluated. That question needs a causal test, which this work keeps separate.
